Wants to go to Europe. Searches the map, calls a car, chooses the special-fare option—cheaper. Before long he is here: the Mediterranean coast. Two walls enclose a narrow lane, bricks covered in latex paint.

The Middle Ages. Production was limited then; every brick had to be moulded by hand, so the flaws were considerable. Look at these useless bastards—ignorant, without desire, accustomed to doing a half-arsed job—serfs auctioned back and forth, with no love whatsoever for the science of brickmaking. Perhaps his mother was a maid in the manor. One night thirty years earlier, a cavalry company had camped nearby and the landlord had warmly invited the officers to call (along with a few common soldiers, the logistics captain’s cousin and nephew). The ceiling on this young labourer’s destiny was not low. He called himself the captain’s son; a lieutenant’s family would have been respectable too. He never associated with the other lower orders, never added them on WeChat—or if he did, never gave them a contact name. He simply liked being difficult. At night he never lay down in the stable with his companions. The bricks he made had the same terrible sense of humour as the tax farmer’s face. That achievement earned him a privilege: the master allowed the young labourer to sleep on a brick mould, braced against the warehouse door—every night, his body pressed against a face not yet formed.

But history never writes only one version. Or perhaps the craftsman woke and discovered he had been born in some self-governing city near Genoa. The sea wind of capitalism’s dawn was gathering strength, preparing to ruin poor bastards across Asia, Africa and Latin America—in space and time, physically and psychologically—and awaken the servility in their hearts. In any case, he came to his senses. “So I’m a free man, for fuck’s sake.” He immediately went off and poured down several drinks, then woke again a century later. The sea wind was still blowing. The ancestral brick mould leaned crookedly beside the door. His grandfather had spent half a lifetime rising from peasant to craftsman, then bestowed upon himself the surname Ferraro. At the same moment, Britons were giving themselves the name Smith. The mould was so battered and old the craftsman felt annoyed each time he saw it. Ferraro let nature take its course as he mixed the concrete, leaving honeycombed hollows in the new brick like a heart webbed with blood vessels.

Times changed. Life grew richer. People tired of pursuing perfection. The fashion now is to reproduce imperfection perfectly. Some progress, at least. Audiences can no longer endure soldiers in armour glittering like stage costumes, faces spotless, hair neither greasy nor matted; nor Eastern Han peasants with strings of dried maize hanging in their homes (maize did not reach China until the late Ming). They have begun checking whether the medals on a uniform conform to regulations, whether the details of a weapon have been faithfully restored.

A notice is pasted to the latex paint. It says: *Know shame, then take courage. Hengda Film City has listened extensively to public opinion and audience feedback, and will strive to restore the filth of history, its flying dust, its starved yellow faces, and the Little Ice Age.*

While scouting locations the director had wondered: “Can a film studio use technology and ideas from the shoulders of giants to present imperfection with even greater perfection? When an imperfect presentation presents imperfection, is it also laying another brick in perfection?”

Some would rather pursue imperfection and preserve every last fraction of it, excavating symbols of damage in the wilderness, taking souvenirs of original sin, prejudice, self-aggrandisement and after-the-fact excuses as their sacred rule, passing on the spirit of imperfection—printed on paper, uploaded to the cloud, preferably stored on a portable hard drive. Others treat life as banishment from death. Neuromedicine, cytology, brain science. The two ends of the axis are perfection and imperfection: from seeking the mystery of death to anaesthetising yourself with future possibilities, where are you? Somewhere in the middle, perhaps. Social media and video-site comment sections are forever at sword point, forcing everyone to choose a side. Every person and every act deserves abuse.

This was not the atmosphere the director wanted. The script was not yet settled, but passion already surged through his mind. Heroes arriving from every direction did not contradict one another: “throughout all ages; ancient and modern combined; Confucian outside, Legalist within; China and the West joined; Chinese learning as substance, Western learning for application.” These phrases tempted him more than a litter of kittens at play. Istanbul, where Eastern and Western civilisations meet, has many cats. Balance, yin and yang, the *Daodejing*, Laozi… The instant he unconsciously named his protagonist “K” (Wang Er would have been better), another timeless scroll entered the world. The simple philosophy inside his head believed there must be a necessary connection running from the doctrine of the mean to synthesis, to the connection of all things, to communion with the universe, to the Gaia consciousness of the Earth, to all things embracing the One, to the many returning to one, to nihilism.

“Fuck me, I knew all this shit was connected. The wisdom of our ancestors! The past is the future, the future is the past, four dimensions, five dimensions, Murphy’s law, the law of attraction, sexual repression, psychoanalysis, Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel.”

Non-professional actors, long takes, modern poetry, dialect—use perfection’s imperfect darlings to create perfection, because perfection is imperfection and imperfection is perfection, one is two, the Two Forms give birth to the Four Images. “I admit I do this to conceal my mediocrity. Mediocrity is the doctrine of the mean, the doctrine of the mean is synthesis and also the connection of all things and also communion with the universe and also the Gaia consciousness of the Earth and also all things embracing the One and also the many returning to one and also nihilism.” The director silently encouraged himself. Since childhood he had suffered from the terrifying disease of logic. Once exposed, you quarantined at home. No choice: watching the washing machine churn water became your only entertainment. The north wind formed a vortex between the densely packed towers of a resettlement housing estate and sent leaves spinning.

EXT. 1 — MEDITERRANEAN-STYLE TOWN. Thick white walls, blue domes, small windows, narrow lanes, wooden shutters. Day. Overcast. No excrement in the street. A curator awkwardly performs an action unbecoming of his position.

The camera wears a grey-blue filter. From three metres away, a medium shot presents him to the audience from the belt up. K is a curator. As for the definition of a curator and how one might prove or falsify it, the screenwriter can only offer inspiration. Find another path: explain it through documentary narration in the voice of Zhao Zhongxiang; reflect it indirectly, with sophistication; establish the background through costume and styling; or make the protagonist say it himself. All of that demands greater care from the director. We are writing a novel. Two words are enough: *a curator*. Anything a novel can explain in a few words is bound to become a symbol; reader and writer alike expect motives and meanings behind it. Only now do I realise I have fallen into my own trap, and the novel’s—not that I pursue symbols, symbols of life symbols of death symbols of will. They pursue me. A storyteller need not care whether grains of sand rise unevenly from the thick white wall, or worry whether the shutters pull upward or downward. He drew in the collar of his trench coat and suddenly felt a little cold.

But creators are now forced to shoulder the responsibility of turning everything into a symbol, converting what lies beyond words into images and inserting them properly into the work. Deliberately avoiding imagery does no good. You try to refuse the thought; at that moment it has already become a symbol of some kind. When you refuse to give the shutters meaning, do the possibilities call up a memory? Sunlight may enter the room at any angle. Perhaps the slanting light painting zebra stripes across the floor represents regret… A non-metaphorical symbol can only represent resistance to metaphor; refusing metaphor symbolises refusal. Perhaps I cannot escape these associations. I am simply less and less certain which of them serves which.

When I describe the curator in such a state of disarray, do I begin from a prejudice, taking it for granted that the protagonist is meant to be a civilised and respectable man? My curator does not actively create value. He drifts within an organisational framework, the entire Leviathan at his back, protected from disease, hunger and upheaval. The character ought to look down from on high. His speciality is abstracting symbols into symbols, passing them from left hand to right. Failing that, he can isolate the concept of “art,” set “artist” beside occupations such as “carpenter,” “cook” and “sanitation worker,” then mock artists for harming society. Or he can split hairs, defining some matter from a predetermined position. Perhaps this alone is enough to make the reader fear him.

While I am over here resolving the curator’s existential crisis, he is over there looking around—the ground lies beneath a skin of seawater, making people lift their legs unconsciously as they walk. It looks slippery. The holiday-resort filter makes the place resemble a Second World War position. The protagonist wears a grey rainproof trench coat, 130 centimetres long (the Albert Camus kind), black wool trousers with a centre crease, and a Republican-era student cap to set off his East Asian face and average height, prompting bullet comments of “spy drama” and “Yu Zecheng”—a spy-drama hero. Seawater surges from every corner and turns ninety degrees along the walls. Cold air from Siberia: you feel its chill only when you tilt your ear to listen. It drains all strength; after thinking for a moment, you have to stop. Wood in air that does not circulate. In films the air is often fake. The audience beyond the screen does not care, but the protagonist suffers badly from it. Every open set is a block of tofu. The world moves with the person, never changing, and leaves him unable to breathe.

SCENE 2. Day. A pitch-black doorway.

The curator walks in. More than a dozen crews shoot here every month. The place began as an inn built for a period boys’-love production. Tiles cover the roof; the only stone object is a horse trough. In the main hall stand eight pairs of fir tables and chairs. An attendant in a coarse linen undershirt keeps tossing a copper coin. The staircase faces the entrance. Upstairs are guest rooms, boxed lunches and contracts. This time the room has been dressed as a dim tavern filled with long tables. Two oak saloon doors will be added in post-production. The customers have changed from down-and-out swordsmen and followers of the demonic path into ninth-rank officials in second-hand mink and liberals. Replace the crowd with a GIF and save some money. Look carefully: the people around him move in three-second loops—the hands about to clap never meet, the mouthfuls of meat are never fully chewed.

He takes an empty seat. Someone naturally edges up beside him holding a glass of beer. He is the only real background actor here, paid 120 yuan a day. The extra had been leaning against a post in a brown coat and tight trousers, his head withdrawn into his collar. A layer of mouldy-orange air hangs in the bar, stinging the eyes. He and the timber watch the curator enter, then abruptly separate, as if the wooden pillar has given birth to a son.

The extra sits too, wriggling for ages until his crowded testicles are properly pressed beneath his crotch. One hand rummages inside his coat as though stealing his own belongings. At last he digs a wad of money from the inner pocket and—smack—flings it onto the table. The money is not slapped down and not thrown down. The rear half meets the table first, then the front describes an arc shaped like the birth rate and—smack—lands as well.

New notes, straight from an ATM. Unfortunately, they have sat too long and the banknotes’ dim, seductive sheen has already slipped away. The curator raises a hand without thinking, wanting to turn one over and see whether the serial numbers run in sequence. He looks around. No one cares about them. His fingers have just touched the money when the man opposite wriggles again, as though adjusting the angle of his underwear. The curator jumps and snatches back his hand.

The extra leans forward. His opening words are: “Mountain against river, Yunnan, Guizhou, Sichuan… Chongqing against Chang’an.”

The money is a fresh peach covered in fine, translucent white fuzz. After touching it, the curator feels an itch inside his fingers. Banknotes stacked together are extremely sharp, easily cutting several invisible slits into the pads of his fingers. The peach fuzz drifts into them. The itch becomes unbearable. He puts his hand in his mouth and sucks several times, hoping to ease the touch of money as if drawing out a wooden splinter.

The director prompts his protagonist just in time through the Bluetooth earpiece: “Tell him: Wangfujing, Jiefang Road; reason or no reason, let us hear Judge Cui.” The curator repeats it faithfully.

The man says: “Dawn arrives in silence. Why are you still so at ease? Run, run, K, you child…”

K again. Anyone steeped for years in Franz Kafka’s novels instinctively names a character K. Kafka: that thunderous name immediately lays a framework over the work and directs its development. Naturally this scene is no ordinary scene. It has depth and substance, the spirit of Kafka and the substance of Kafka—that thing no one can describe.

“Tell him: Don’t trouble yourself.” The director issues his instruction at exactly the right moment.

K lowers his voice and asks, “Why doesn’t this one rhyme? Aren’t these countersigns?”

From the earpiece comes a half-formed *mm*, the dull plosive of words that reach the tip of the tongue and are pulled back. Then a hiss of breath, as though a cutscene has been triggered: some enormously complicated and time-consuming mission, a vast adventure, an epic conspiracy, is about to begin, and the director, as guide, will have to shoulder responsibility. Air leaks from his lungs through nose and mouth. A few dozen seconds of mental activity feel like two days spent carrying bricks.

This spirited young director could have, and indeed had, planned it this way—routine procedure, meaning that the producer would as usual deliver the repeated script and storyboards to the assistant through various media: sometimes white letters on black, sometimes an electronic copy by email, sometimes wrapped as a birthday present (hidden beneath a leather cover and disguised as an expensive notebook). Once, just as the assistant cut open the belly of a fish, guess what happened?

The assistant would then, rigorously and in complete accordance with process and workplace rules, convert the script into a lawful official format and present it to the director. The director was accustomed to reciting lines from the page to obedient actors. Now he regretted it. *How the fuck did improvisation get in here? If I’d known, I wouldn’t have invited this son of a bitch to make a cameo.* With no alternative, he drained his whole body of strength to address the curator in a drowsy voice, a voice like the whisper of the sea wind: “There is a principle… in principle, you are not allowed to ask questions…”

He does not say what he wants to say. It is all the author’s fault for being too stupid; everything can only be entrusted to dreams, to great sleep, to Jia Baoyu wandering through the Land of Illusion in *Dream of the Red Chamber*.

Last night, the curator’s dream lasted until noon. The director had said he wanted him in a cameo, and the curator had been excited all day. That night he dreamt of the director. His dreams are all quite ordinary: mysterious, with room left over for interpretation and metaphor. The dream staged a non-logical coherence and a coherent non-logic, half true, half false. Before he could gather himself, what he had encountered the night before was not a collection of fragmentary scenes, not certain moments stretched without limit, replayed, savoured without limit, laden with causes and consequences and possibilities—profound moments that make the waking person hold his piss desperately, press his brain into service, force it to remember the plot, while at the same time trying to shut it down by force and carry you back into the great rest—a lovely night, or regret beyond repair.

The dream stops here and extends at the same time. An actor’s dream has a beginning and an end. It stops in endless waiting, waiting for the next instruction. The heavy breathing at the other end of the earpiece gradually fades. There is no clock in the bar. Customers, bartender, kitchen staff: every human movement repeats at three-second intervals. The stone ceiling cuts off the course of sun and moon. Time contracts inside an endless instant. The curator has nothing to do. Might as well watch Bilibili.

*A Utopia of Goats and Horses*—a kitten appears and disappears among weeds taller than a person. So cute. A goat lowers its head to eat the grass and does not resist the carnivore beside it, allowing the kitten to jump onto its back. The long hair with a few scraps of leaf caught in it must be soft and warm. The kitten seems to remember its white mother, and a sudden melancholy fills its brain. Perhaps it does not understand the feeling. On the goat’s back, its paws extend and retract as it kneads. The fleece is thick enough that the cat cannot hurt it. At this moment the goat is immersed in rumination, eyes and mouth both turning. A horse drenched in sweat comes close, sprays the rising steam over the cat, strokes it with its chin, presses the broad side of its head towards it, wanting intimacy with this little thing. The mountain is made of stone. Grass grows in a thin layer of soil, its roots fighting the rock at every moment, spreading everywhere and finding no passage. It wants to live too. Photosynthesis alone will not do, especially when the sun inside the screen has no warmth. You guessed correctly again: it is a texture map. But the grass must live. Every blade, every goat and cat must live. Its body spreads helplessly. If it cannot go down it strains upwards; the air contains moisture too. “Have study and work been feeling stressful lately?” the director says. “Today I’m recommending an incredibly useful tool.” The curator is not under much pressure. The kitten seems to remember something—perhaps its white mother again. It looks alertly across its home, ears tipped slightly, attention caught by some fresh disturbance in the grass. In an instant it jumps to the ground and sniffs the earth with all its strength.

“Cats like horses,” the narrator says slowly. The sentence comes from far away, drifting from very, very far away, from beyond the horizon, yet it belongs to you alone and can reach only your ears, every word intact. Labiodentals, affricates, a trace of a Beijing accent—he is professionally trained. His voice breaks the laws of nature and vibrates your temples. Every line of narration has the intimacy of soothing a pet. He knows how to fill the piece missing from a person—pets lack order; with us, it is precisely the opposite.

The curator loves animal documentaries. Their conscientious directors seek neither fame nor profit, wishing only to record an instant of life. This undoubtedly deserves respect. They knew from the beginning that pirated copies would travel the world, perhaps with the watermark of a gambling site stamped in the lower left and the QQ number of a nude-chat scam in the upper right, but they do not care. Nature, at least, is real.

“Much better than those films that make no fucking sense.” The curator wants to post to WeChat Moments. A rebellion without cause makes him want to complain. He writes the post, deletes it, then thinks of something catchier: “I prefer animals to exercise; ecology to ideology.” Less than three seconds after posting, he refreshes the screen several times and deletes it again. He is not afraid of embarrassment, only of being mistaken for someone who still cares about these things.

A pop-up. WeChat. His mate sends a location: Minle Mutton Soup, behind Xingnong Square. He writes something. They have arranged to eat and discuss an art exhibition the curator will soon organise in a two-storey garden villa in the French Concession. The venue’s name sits beneath his tongue, but for the moment he cannot remember it. Fortunately, everyone knows the nickname. At the words *villa*, *fucking impressive*, *art*, listener and speaker arrive at a shared understanding. “Oh, that place, on the same latitude as the Austro-Hungarian embassy.” Ambiguity is also a form of tacit agreement, used to mark the blurriest house on the map. If you describe a piece of real estate through “linear narrative,” it faces south with its back to the north; its central axis can extend through Heilongjiang, then Vladivostok, then farther north… And now we are writing symbols again. What does this house represent? What do you think it represents? Perhaps it represents nothing but the time of yours it has wasted, wasting your time through cheap little traps of thought made by playing with the Chinese pronouns for he, she and it. Reading this novel is a waste of time. Is your time worth wasting? The world is fast. People were already making psychoanalytic thrillers during the Second World War. Writing like this now is truly useless, no progress at all.

Apparently a company recently built some kind of facial electromyography sensor that can decode particular sounds in dreams and transmit dream language in both directions—words are sent through the device to sleeping subjects, who wake able to repeat them in full. Meaning that in future you may have to watch advertisements even in your dreams. Only members will be allowed to dream without ads.

Yesterday he made a cameo in his good mate’s film. Every event conformed to reason, so naturally the curator ought to retain correct knowledge and memory. He has not forgotten his identity, the plan, the organisation, technology making everything more convenient, communication through dreams… and the Bluetooth earpiece. “Send a voice message complaining: Ah, another commercial district. Nothing to eat but the same shitty things, expensive and disgusting.” The director speaks through the earpiece. “Your tone must be impatient.” After such a long silence, the curator had forgotten the director. The man belonged, after all, to last night’s dream, a sword from a fallen dynasty. The director too seems to wake from a dream. He hastens to make up for his absence and begins issuing exact instructions—tone and gaze, body language, all must meet the requirements. He calls himself a craftsman.

His mate says helplessly, “What’s wrong with a central kitchen, *li*? You should count yourself lucky there’s food at all. Most people pay a premium for pre-made dishes.” This gentleman came from the north and is something of a noted connoisseur. Everyone loves food these days; livestream eaters have already eaten the globe on our behalf. He, however, remains devoted, giving tireless love to his favourite mutton soup. Great cities gather migrants and mutton soups from every direction. The trinity of the writer’s faith is Shanxian mutton soup, Pingquan mutton soup and Cangzhou mutton soup; Hailar mutton soup is honoured as the Virgin Mary, while Ningxia mutton soup may receive an exalted title such as King Solomon. At this, the curator almost laughs. “Why do you sound like classical European literature?” Even the introduction of his friend has become stiff and formal, going so far as to call him “this gentleman.” “And that *li*. Who talks like you…”

Like me, he sits leaning against the sofa for a while. Time circulates through the bathroom exhaust fan. He does not know what to write.

Might as well watch Bilibili. The camera pulls back again, into depth. Like milk poured over coffee, the harmonious field of dark green, light green and faded grey-green is suddenly broken. On the flat top of a cylindrical rock mountain, we see a building growing abruptly, from a distance like the little house and balloon inside an eye-test machine. The high ground is a utopia too. Another group of kittens, goats and horses: enlightened, they disdain to join the herd of their own kind—those who pursue only the basic security of safety and a full stomach. They long ago discarded the nonhuman written form of the pronoun “they,”<button class="note-ref" type="button" data-note="In written Chinese, the homophones 他们 and 它们 distinguish human ‘they’ from nonhuman ‘they’.">※</button> trading hard work for stability. Their house is extraordinarily complex in its construction, what the animals call anthro-mimetics, inspired by a camera gimbal.

The little house is built inside two nested metal frames, with a large gyroscopic apparatus at its core. The device consists of a high-speed rotor and a precision bearing system capable of sensing external changes in time. During an earthquake, the Coriolis force generated by the spinning gyroscope can counteract the seismic waves and automatically stabilise the house. The animals draw power from solar panels and windmills. Insulation, high-efficiency windows and intelligent lighting fill the home. There are also 360-degree panoramic windows and an unobstructed natural view. The kitten and his white mother both love the sun.

With such a fine natural display case, the director has struck valuable material. Who would still go to a zoo? The camera never leaves. But we must consider reality—the hooves of goats and horses did not evolve into shapes capable of using tools, and the fickle kitten cannot perform most precision work. This leaves faults in the gyroscope, which now and then rehearses an earthquake. The animals have therefore acquired the habits of sailors: aboard a deck in heavy seas, they shuffle their feet and stand at right angles to keep from falling, rather like the ritual gait known as the Yu Step. The same footwork appears in boat boxing, once widespread in the Wu–Yue region. One can only call it nature’s miraculous convergent evolution.

It does not end there. This gait flowered and spread through later generations. The curator knows plenty of people who make art or make work and walk this way. I know them too. Their work resembles the goats’ house, a battered mechanical sphere. Or resembles something. I thought for ages and could not say what. Perhaps power that exists only in order to exist, survives only in order to survive. The things they create have no aim except to keep themselves from falling out of the system.

With no other choice, the curator performs his secret technique. He performs it, mind you, not me. The truth is the curator knows a move for retracting his genitals into his abdomen. In Istanbul he learnt it from a Persian juggler with the face of a porcelain doll. As he remembers, the man was both old and young. Long lashes flashed black; his hair curled more sharply than his lashes, drawing the tourists’ gaze towards green irises. He was concentrating, staring at his curled Ottoman moustache—working the art. The little moustache rose at the same angle as the lashes. Its tips had been deliberately waxed into the shape of McDonald’s arches.

It is a long story. The curator listened for a long time too—listened to the Persian juggler speak of his Turkic ancestors. A great art such as retracting the genitals deserves preparation, time and practice. The secret technique came from the messengers of Khwarazm. Do not misunderstand: this is man and nature, not a shallow political metaphor. “As everyone knows, the king of that country liked only good news, and so established the world’s first system for monitoring public opinion. Inside Samarkand he was omniscient and omnipotent. No internet, no electricity; control through the most primitive method, fear. Every messenger bringing bad news would be castrated, yet they had no choice but to report the enemy’s latest movements—which khan had defected, who had been beheaded by the Mongols… At the moment of national survival, the messengers had no choice but to sacrifice themselves…” The juggler’s voice sank lower and lower, as if the matter were deeply shameful. “Things are better now. Next time, ah, I’ll vote for Erdoğan again. You know, so many young people now understand nothing about economics or international relations, all they want is change, but who knows what it might change into…”

As the juggler talked, he wandered off the subject. The curator became anxious and hurriedly said, No, no, no, no, no politics. “Then… the messengers began rigorous practice of the art of retracting the genitals. When their news was bad, they retracted before seeing the king, sobbing in court that an enemy state had imprisoned and castrated them. Once the emperor saw that, he could hardly lose his temper. He waved a hand and dismissed them. From then on, the messengers pretending to be eunuchs enjoyed freedom of speech. Every day, like muttering ghosts, they brought news of land and people lost. In any case, the Mongols eventually destroyed Khwarazm, didn’t they? That was that. Some people fled west, all the way to what is now Turkey.” The curator later studied under him. He no longer remembers how he learnt it.

Of course, I could also write that the curator is a castrate, an impotent man, a eunuch. In the manner of Mo Yan, I could invent a coarse myth about sex: perhaps someone sat on his root and broke it; perhaps he thrust too hard from behind, rammed the other person’s coccyx and snapped it again. I could directly describe the curator’s sexual weakness and deliver a wild passage of bodily writing. But what use would that be? To show that I have severed myself from patriarchy? Would it truly help? The motives of writing always protest too much. Every woman draws men’s attention; some simply restrain themselves and impersonate human beings. When they cannot, they find excuses—I am too old; I have a family. Unless I say that I am K, the curator is K too, weak in body and likewise in spirit. Better not make him a eunuch. Since ancient times, castrated men have been the worst of all. Depriving eunuchs of sex only encourages aggression.

He looks into the black doorway. So many plastic signs, absorbing light: nail salon, cat café, guitar lessons, tattoos and facial piercings, murder-mystery games. Too many. Must not let them cheat you of money. Several layers of black plastic sheeting left behind after renovation cover the front of the building as a welcome carpet. The lobby is packed. Four lifts fight up and down but can never carry everyone: those rushing to spend and those rushing to earn are equally frantic. The curator squeezes in and asks someone to press twenty-seven. Why so long? The writer asks the same question. He did not delay on purpose. This is what happened: the lift stopped at every floor and new people entered and left each time. This was normal. Six rhyming safety rules were written on the wall. Four screens played advertisements, each one screaming a slogan hoarse. Two bars designed to block electric bikes made uncomfortable seats. The ads were deafening; the brain struggled to classify them as white noise. The curator was still holding his genitals inside.
